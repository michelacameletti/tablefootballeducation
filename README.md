# Table Football for Statistics Education



<p align="center"> <img src="tablefootball_logo.png" alt="Table Football and Statistics" width="300"> </p>

This repository contains the Excel workbooks and supporting materials associated with the paper:

> Michela Cameletti and Rodolfo Metulini, *Using Table Football to Introduce Statistical Reasoning and Predictive Modeling to High-School Students*.
> 
The project uses table football as a game-based context for introducing conditional probability, uncertainty, statistical prediction, and the limitations of statistical models. The materials are designed for university outreach activities with high-school students, but they can also be adapted for introductory statistics courses.

## Overview

The repository supports two complementary activities:

1. **Individual-shot prediction**: students take shots under different playing conditions and compare the observed outcome with the probability of scoring estimated by a logistic regression model.
2. **Team-match prediction**: two-player teams play a match, and a probability of victory is generated by a logistic regression model after the first goal has been scored.

The logistic models were estimated from data collected across university outreach events, including science fairs, open days, and orientation activities and should be regarded as convenience samples rather than representative samples of table-football players.
The fitted models are intended primarily as educational tools. Their outputs should not be interpreted as formally validated predictions of table-football performance. Estimated associations should not be interpreted as causal effects.

## Repository contents

The repository includes the following files:

- `individual_shots.xlsx`: data, fitted logistic regression model, and prediction interface for the individual-shot activity;
- `team_matches.xlsx`: data, fitted logistic regression model, and prediction interface for the team-match activity;


## Individual-shot activity

### Statistical unit

One observation corresponds to one shot.

### Outcome

- `goal = 1`: the shot results in a goal;
- `goal = 0`: the shot does not result in a goal.

### Information used by the model

The fitted model uses information on:

- age category;
- gender;
- type of school attended;
- shooting position: central or angled;
- defensive configuration: defenders present or absent.

The workbook returns the estimated conditional probability

`P(goal | player characteristics, shot characteristics)`.

The probability is shown before the shot is taken. After the outcome is observed, the workbook can calculate

`Delta = observed outcome - predicted probability`.

For a sequence of shots, the average value of `Delta` can be used as a descriptive measure of performance relative to the probabilities generated by the model.

## Team-match activity

### Statistical unit

One observation corresponds to one match between Team A and Team B. Each team consists of two players, usually assigned to the roles of attacker and defender.

### Outcome

- `Team A win = 1`: Team A wins the match;
- `Team A win = 0`: Team B wins the match.

### Information used by the model

The model is based on differences between Team A and Team B in:

- average age;
- average self-assessed table-football skill;
- average frequency of play during the previous year;
- number of players competing in their preferred role;
- number of female players.

The model also includes an indicator identifying which team scored the first goal.

The workbook returns the estimated in-play probability

`P(Team A wins | team characteristics, first goal)`.

This probability is generated immediately after the first goal, while the final match outcome is still unknown. The complementary probability corresponds to Team B winning.

After the match, the observed outcome can be compared with the predicted probability through

`Delta = observed outcome - predicted probability`.

## How to use the workbooks

1. Download the required Excel workbook.
2. Open it in Microsoft Excel and enable editing.
3. Read the instructions provided in the workbook before entering new data.
4. Enter the participant, shot, team, or match characteristics in the designated input cells.
5. Read the predicted probability returned by the fitted model.
6. After the shot or match has ended, enter the observed outcome.
7. Use the comparison between prediction and outcome to discuss uncertainty, variability, and information not included in the model.

The input cells, output cells, and protected formula cells should be clearly identified in each workbook.

## Excel Solver

The logistic regression models were estimated in Excel by maximizing the log-likelihood with the Solver add-in.

Solver is not required to generate predictions from the already fitted models. It is required only when users wish to re-estimate the model after modifying or extending the dataset.

To enable Solver in Excel:

1. open **File > Options > Add-ins**;
2. select **Excel Add-ins** and click **Go**;
3. select **Solver Add-in**;
4. click **OK**.

When re-estimating a model, users should verify that the objective cell, coefficient cells, and optimization settings correspond to those described in the workbook instructions.

## Suggested educational use

A laboratory session can be organized around the following sequence:

1. introduce probability and conditional probability using simple examples or two-way tables;
2. explain that a statistical model combines available information to produce a probability rather than a certain prediction;
3. generate the probability for a shot or match;
4. observe the actual outcome;
5. compare the prediction with the outcome;
6. discuss why a high-probability event may fail to occur and why a low-probability event may still occur;
7. identify relevant factors that were not measured or included in the model.

The workbooks can be used independently.
